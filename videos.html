Research you can watch

A paper is written for the referee. Almost nobody else reads it, including most mathematicians in neighbouring fields, and that is a loss on both sides: the ideas stay locked up, and the people who might use them never hear about them. I am turning my research, and the research I find most exciting, into videos that a graduate student, an engineer, or a curious person with some mathematics can follow from beginning to end.

The rule for every video is the same. Start with the question in plain words, and say why anyone cares. Show the obvious approach and exactly where it breaks. Then show the idea that works, with the pictures the paper did not have room for, and end with what is still open. No theorem is stated before you know why you would want it.

What the channel will carry

One video per paper. Every paper on this site gets a talk, ten to twenty minutes, with the PDF linked underneath. The cards below track which ones exist.

How the proof works. Longer, blackboard-style walks through a single argument for people who want the details: the Marchenko–Pastur pruning theorem, the sharp exponent for least singular values, the doubling-and-jamming construction.

AI doing mathematics. Episodes on the math brain project: what happens when AI agents are pointed at open problems, what they get right, what they get wrong, and what a machine-checked proof actually looks like. This is the part of the channel where the research is happening as it is being filmed.

Open problems, explained. Short pieces on a single open problem from probability, random matrices or dynamics: what it asks, why it is hard, and what would count as progress. The point is to make the frontier visible to people who are not yet standing on it.

Who it is for

Students deciding what to work on, researchers in adjacent fields, engineers who use these tools without the theory, and anyone who wants to see what current mathematics looks like from the inside.

What it is not

Not a lecture course and not a popular-science gloss. Real theorems, real proofs, at the pace of a good seminar talk, with the pictures and the honesty about what is unfinished.

Why video

A proof has a shape, and shapes are easier to show than to write. Twenty minutes with a drawing does what forty pages cannot: it lets you see why the argument had to go that way.
