Teaching statement

Back in 2017, during my first week teaching at Yeshiva University, I made what I initially thought was a major misstep. While explaining the chain rule, I got so enthusiastic about a particular example that I completely derailed my planned lecture to spend 20 minutes discussing “why” the rule works rather than just “how” to use it. I was sure I’d made a mistake – until a student approached me after class to say it was the first time calculus had ever made sense to him. That moment informed my approach to teaching mathematics.

Over the years, I have taught at multiple levels and institutions, from high school to college classrooms at Yeshiva University and Penn State. My experience ranges from teaching Algebra 1 and 2 to teenagers to teaching probability theory and mathematical statistics to undergraduates. In each environment, I strive to bring value, motivated by the profound impact I believe teaching has on students’ lives and on the world. The excitement I feel for teaching mathematics has only grown with time, driving me to always seek ways to make these subjects clearer, more accessible, and inspiring for my students.

I love teaching math, and sharing foundational mathematical concepts with hundreds of students each year feels like one of the most meaningful contributions I can make. When I teach, I feel a genuine excitement, almost as if I’m performing, and I think carefully about different ways to explain these concepts. Even something as straightforward as understanding the functions sin and cos can be taught through rote memorization or through a deeper exploration of projections and why mathematicians find these concepts so intriguing.

Sometimes, my students think I’m a bit quirky. I often bring in random objects to demonstrate ideas—once, I used a slinky to illustrate wave functions, and it seemed to help them understand why waves are so essential in mathematics. Mathematics isn’t a dry, formal subject confined to textbooks. It’s dynamic and sometimes a bit chaotic, and truly understanding it often involves breaking out of traditional molds.

I’ll be honest—I’m not the world’s most organized teacher. My whiteboard usually ends up covered in a chaotic mix of calculations, diagrams, and stray notes. But I’ve found that this actually helps students view mathematics as a process rather than a collection of neat, pre-packaged solutions. And yes, I always post nice clean notes for the students to follow. When working through a proof in class, I intentionally share my false starts and dead ends. “See?” I’ll tell them, “Even I get stuck sometimes. This isn’t failure—it’s just part of doing math.”

Teaching multivariable calculus at Penn State taught me something crucial: students don’t just need to learn the material—they need to believe they can learn it. A significant part of my job is convincing them that, yes, they absolutely can understand this material. I want them to feel confident, to know that if they make an effort, they will succeed in solving problems that might initially seem insurmountable.

Over the years, I’ve observed something interesting—the students who truly understand the material aren’t always the ones who solve problems the fastest. They’re the ones who can explain why their solution works. For that reason, I encourage my students to discuss mathematics. In my calculus classes, students are expected to explain concepts to one another. Sure, it takes more time than merely practicing procedures, but the depth of understanding they gain is invaluable.

The longer I teach, the more I realize how much teaching is about trust. Students need to trust that it’s okay to be confused, to ask “simple” questions, and to struggle. It’s okay—even preferable—to learn through exploration and discussion rather than mere memorization. I always encourage them to create visual representations of what they’re doing so that their intuition becomes grounded. Even when tutoring in real analysis, I make sure my students visualize different topologies, what open sets look like, and how various types of functions might behave. For humans, a picture is worth a thousand words; in mathematics, it might be worth even more.

Seven years into teaching, I’m still excited before each class. Will today’s explanation resonate? Will the new example I thought of actually clarify the concept? This nervous energy keeps me engaged, continually thinking about how to make each lesson better than the last. Ultimately, teaching mathematics is more than just transferring knowledge—it’s about sharing the excitement, the beauty, and sometimes even the frustration of mathematical discovery.
