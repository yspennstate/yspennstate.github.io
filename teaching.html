How I teach

Students arrive with different preparation, so I start by finding out what they can already do and then give them a concrete problem that makes the next idea necessary. I ask them to say why a method applies, which assumptions it uses, and what happens when one of those assumptions changes. In probability this matters most: a student who can say exactly where independence or a moment condition was used can also see when a result will fail.

I assess with short oral explanations, counterexamples and calculations a student must be able to reproduce, so that a fluent answer cannot stand in for understanding. When AI tools are part of a course, students first develop and defend their own argument and only then compare it with a generated one, looking for the step where the two part ways.

Courses I can teach

Probability, random matrices, high-dimensional statistics and the mathematical foundations of machine learning; calculus, linear algebra and discrete mathematics. I would also like to teach a course on checked computation and AI-assisted proof, in which students attach sources and exact hypotheses to claims, find an incorrect argument, and trace what changes when a premise is corrected.

Student projects

I keep projects small and checkable: a precise question, a reproducible starting point, and a result that counts whether it is a theorem, a verified computation or a well-documented negative finding. Examples: extreme singular values of random submatrices, spiked random matrices, certified numerics in statistical physics, and retrieval experiments on Math Brain.
