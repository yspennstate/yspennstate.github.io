Research

Random matrices, neural networks, and operator algebras

Most of my work sits where random matrix theory meets something else: the weight matrices of a deep network, the stability of phase retrieval, the discrepancy of structured matrix families, or the boundary spectrum of a topological insulator. The proofs tend to end in a number or a threshold, and where a claim has a finite core I like to have a computer check it.

In more detail

Noise, signal, and the line between them

A trained neural network is a stack of matrices, and each one is structure buried in noise. The Marchenko–Pastur law says what the noise looks like, so whatever lies outside that bulk is signal. Most of my work on deep learning comes from that: a pruning rule that makes networks smaller and often more accurate, used on Vision Transformers trained on ImageNet; a way to track randomness leaving the weights during training; and theorems on when accuracy is stable, when a random ReLU network is non-expansive, and when a classifier must have adversarial neighbours.

In probability the same questions get sharper. How small can the least singular value of a Gaussian matrix be made by choosing which rows to keep, and what does that say about phase retrieval? What happens to spiked models when the rank grows with the dimension? Does the Matrix Spencer conjecture hold for Toeplitz and Hankel matrices, and what are the moments of their limit laws? Related methods verified the Krauth–Mézard capacity of the binary perceptron with computer-checked certificates.

The third thread, from my time with Emil Prodan, is operator algebras and K-theory: bulk-boundary correspondence for resonator arrays patterned by a dynamical system, and the Higson–Prodan universal Chern Hamiltonians extended to surfaces with boundary by doubling and jamming, with the interface class computed in the Roe algebra.

I also work in dynamical systems and combinatorics, where I started: fixed points and horseshoes found by labelling the corners of cubes, and full chaos shown to be generic among non-autonomous interval systems.
