Research

Random matrices, neural networks, and operator algebras

Most of my work sits where random matrix theory meets something else: the weight matrices of a deep network, the stability of phase retrieval, the discrepancy of structured matrix families, or the boundary spectrum of a topological insulator. The proofs tend to end in a number or a threshold, and where a claim has a finite core I like to have a computer check it.

In more detail

Noise, signal, and the line between them

A trained neural network is a stack of matrices, and to a random matrix theorist each of them looks like a familiar object: a small amount of structure buried in noise. The Marchenko–Pastur law says exactly what the noise part should look like, so everything outside that bulk is signal. That observation runs through most of my work on deep learning. It gives a pruning rule that makes networks smaller and often more accurate, which we have taken as far as Vision Transformers trained on ImageNet; a way to watch randomness leave the weights during training; and theorems about when accuracy is stable, when a random ReLU network is globally non-expansive, and when a classifier must have adversarial neighbours.

Pushed into probability proper, the same instinct leads to the questions I find hardest to put down. How small can the least singular value of a Gaussian matrix become when an adversary chooses which rows to keep, and what does the answer say about the stability of phase retrieval? What happens to spiked models when the rank of the signal grows with the dimension? Can the Matrix Spencer conjecture be settled for Toeplitz and Hankel families, and what are the moments of their limit laws? These have sharp answers, and some of them carry over to the capacity of the binary perceptron, whose Krauth–Mézard threshold I verified with computer-checked certificates.

The third thread is operator algebras and K-theory, from my time with Emil Prodan: bulk-boundary correspondence for resonator arrays patterned by a dynamical system, and the universal Chern Hamiltonians of Higson and Prodan, which I extended to surfaces with boundary by doubling the surface and jamming the seam, with the interface class computed in the Roe algebra.

Alongside these I keep a foot in dynamical systems and combinatorics, where I started: finding fixed points and horseshoes by labelling the corners of cubes, and showing that in the space of all non-autonomous interval systems, full chaos is generic. Where randomness enters, as in the edge-of-chaos transition of a randomly initialized autoencoder, that work joins the main line.
