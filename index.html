What I work on

Five threads, one habit

The habit is to take a question people answer by experiment or by folklore, find the mathematical object underneath it, and prove something sharp about that object. The threads are where the habit has taken me.

What I am interested in

Noise, signal, and the line between them

A trained neural network is a stack of matrices, and each of those matrices looks, to a random matrix theorist, like a familiar thing: a small amount of structure buried in a lot of noise. The Marchenko–Pastur law says exactly what the noise part should look like. Everything outside that bulk is signal. That one observation runs through most of my work on deep learning. It gives a pruning rule that makes networks smaller and often more accurate, a way to watch randomness leave the weights during training, and theorems about when accuracy is stable and when a classifier must have adversarial neighbors.

The same instinct, pushed into pure probability, produces the questions I find hardest to put down. How small can the least singular value of a Gaussian matrix become if an adversary picks which rows to keep? What happens to spiked models when the rank of the signal grows with the dimension? Can the Matrix Spencer conjecture be settled for structured families like Toeplitz and Hankel matrices? These have sharp answers, and the answers turn out to matter for phase retrieval, for compressed sensing, and for the capacity of the binary perceptron, whose Krauth–Mézard threshold I recently verified with computer-checked certificates.

Before random matrices there was chaos. My master's work with Marian Gidea found fixed points and horseshoes by labeling the corners of cubes, a Sperner-type argument that replaces index theory with pictures. I have kept returning to that combinatorial way of seeing: a proof of Atanassov's conjecture, a combinatorial Kakutani theorem, and lately a series of papers showing that in the space of all non-autonomous interval systems, full-blown chaos is not rare but generic.

And there is a topological thread, from my time with Emil Prodan: bulk-boundary correspondence for resonator arrays and for the universal Chern Hamiltonians of Higson and Prodan, proved through the K-theory of C*-algebras and, most recently, extended to surfaces with boundary by doubling the surface and jamming the seam.

What ties these together is a taste for problems where the answer is a number or a threshold, and a preference for proofs that a computer can replay.

"Slight differences in initial conditions can cause drastic changes in the long-term results."

How I described chaos in 2017, when the master's work was first presented at Llavefest in Barcelona. Fixed points and periodic orbits are the things that stay put, and that is still the shortest description of why I like the subject.
