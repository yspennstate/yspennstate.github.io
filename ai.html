AI and open problems

Using AI to prove open problems

The idea is simple to state. Mathematics has thousands of well-posed open problems sitting in the back pages of monographs and surveys, most of them untouched since they were written down. AI systems can now do serious mathematics. So build a system that reads those problems, works on them, referees its own work, and never forgets what it learned, then let it run, every day, and advance the field.

Work in progress · release soon

The main idea

A mathematician that never forgets

Most of the difficulty in research is not the hard step in the middle of a proof. It is knowing what has been tried, what is already known, which tools worked on which kinds of problem, and which mistakes you personally keep making. A human mathematician accumulates that over a career. A language model starts from zero every time it is asked a question.

So the project has two halves. The first is a pipeline for open problems: harvest them from the literature with exact sources, attempt them with AI agents, referee each attempt adversarially, check the finite parts by machine, and record the outcome honestly, including the failures. The second is a memory, which I call the math brain, that every agent reads before it starts and writes to when it finishes. The memory is what makes the pipeline compound. An agent working on a Toeplitz problem today knows what an agent learned about Hankel matrices last month, and knows the three ways an earlier attempt went wrong.

The goal is not a leaderboard. It is to settle problems that matter to the people who posed them, publish the ones that survive prior-art checks, and hand the community a tool that makes its own open-problem lists shorter.

In short

Open questions come in from monographs and surveys in probability, random matrices and dynamics, each recorded with its source and page. AI agents attempt them, having first read what the brain knows about related results and past mistakes. A second agent then tries to break each argument, and the finite parts go to Lean 4 or a numerical certificate.

Whatever was learned, whether a proof, a counterexample, a dead end or a reference that closes the problem, goes back into the brain, so the next attempt starts further along than the last one did.

The problem

Strong solvers, no memory

Put a frontier model to work on a research problem and its failures are rarely arithmetic. In the audits I have run, the calculations were almost always right. What goes wrong is subtler and more human: a conclusion phrased more strongly than its derivation supports, a citation that matches a neighbouring object rather than the one in the question, a result counted as new because no search turned up an earlier proof, an inequality that is secretly Markov's inequality in disguise.

Every one of those is caught by reading, not by computing, and every one of them recurs, because a stateless model makes the same mistake fresh each morning. That is the gap. The solver is strong; the memory is missing; and without memory nothing accumulates.

The math brain

What I am building

The system I call the math brain is a memory that AI agents read at the start of every task and write back to at the end. It is not a vector database with a chat interface. It is closer to what a working mathematician carries around: a few thousand notes, an association graph between them, a sense of which results are solid and which are provisional, and a set of scars, the specific ways things went wrong before, that fire on their own when a new situation resembles an old one.

Three ideas do most of the work.

1. Every note carries its own doubt

A note is stored with a status: verified, provisional, superseded by a later note, or an owner-stated rule. When it surfaces, the agent sees the status and how often that note has been useful before. A note is a hint, not a verdict. Obeying notes blindly turns out to be as harmful as ignoring them; the system is calibrated so that it fires less and means more.

2. Recall is a walk, not a lookup

Recall mixes lexical search, semantic search and a short random walk on the association graph. The walk is deliberately short: an unbounded walk mixes to a query-independent law and everything looks like a hub. The useful memory lives before mixing. That is a small theorem about diffusions with killing, and it is the reason the retrieval works.

3. It consolidates while nobody is working

Overnight, a consolidation pass reads the day, merges duplicates, promotes provisional notes that were confirmed, demotes ones that were contradicted, and scores the predictions the system made about what would happen next. Predictions that miss become curiosity: questions the brain carries forward. This is the part that makes it a brain rather than a filing cabinet.

The pipeline

How an open problem moves through it

The problems come from the literature: the open-problem sections of monographs and surveys in probability, random matrix theory and dynamics, each recorded with its source and exact locator so that nothing is attributed to the wrong author. Intake is followed by attempts, and attempts by adversarial refereeing, in which a separate agent is asked to break the argument rather than admire it. Where a claim has a finite core, it is checked in Lean 4 or by a numerical certificate. Where it does not, it is re-derived independently, and the write-up says so. Prior art is checked by comparing the objects by name, not by whether a search came back empty.

Results are labelled honestly. Settled means a written proof that survived refereeing. Partial means the header names what is still unproved. Excluded means the literature already had it, with the reference. The labels are the product as much as the theorems are.

Notes, their links, and a recall walk that stops before it mixes. The colour of a node is its status.

The loop, end to end

Intake

Open problems harvested from monographs and surveys, each with source, page and exact wording.

Recall

The brain surfaces what it knows: related results, prior attempts, and the scars from similar problems.

Attempt

Agents work the problem. Every claim is written with its size of doubt attached.

Referee

A second agent tries to break the argument. Finite cores go to Lean 4 or a numerical certificate.

Remember

What was learned, including what failed, is written back. Overnight the brain consolidates it.

Inside the brain

What a piece of it looks like

Three real excerpts from the running system. The first is a note the brain wrote after an agent's proof was found wrong. The second is the rule it compiled from that note, which now fires on its own whenever a new proof takes the same step. The third is what an agent sees when it asks the brain a question.

1 · A memory note
name: proof_steps_need_derived_joint_structure
type: feedback     status: provisional
description: A proof states conditional expectations
  multiply without joint independence; tower-property
  products and conditional bounds need a derived
  dependence argument.

FAILURE MECHANISM. A proof states that conditional
expectations multiply without deriving the required
joint structure. Conditional mean zero, pairwise
information, or a bound conditioned on a different
sigma-field for each factor does not imply independence.

EVIDENCE. In the stored martingale CLT proof the first
characteristic-function identity was false. For
independent fair signs U, W, let X1 = U and
X2 = (1+U)W: these are martingale differences. At
t = pi/2 the true characteristic function of the sum
is -i; the proposed product is zero. The standard CLT
itself is correct. 48 exact finite-tree controls are
retained with the correction.

PREVENTION. Write the conditioning sigma-field and the
measurable factors on every step. Derive the identity
on a small dependent example first.

A note records the mechanism of a mistake, a concrete counterexample, and what to do instead. The status line says how far to trust it.

2 · The reflex compiled from it
FIRE WHEN a proof states that a product of
  conditional characteristic functions equals the
  characteristic function of the sum merely because
  the increments are martingale differences; or says
  "therefore the conditional bounds multiply into a
  joint probability" with no filtration or
  independence argument.

DO NOT FIRE WHEN independence or a conditional-
  independence structure is explicitly established,
  or the dependence step is honestly left as an
  open obligation.

HINT judged on-domain 1 of 6 times it fired.
  A fire is a question, not a verdict.

Reflexes surface in the agent's context while it works, unasked. Each one carries its own precision, so a noisy reflex is weighed less than a sharp one.

Where it stands

Early results, still under review

The system has been running daily on my own research for several months and on a growing ledger of open problems in probability, random matrices and dynamics since the summer. Several problems from that ledger now have written proofs or counterexamples that passed the internal referee, a few of them with machine-checked cores. I am not listing them here yet. The remaining step, the one that separates a result from a claim, is the prior-art check: pulling the papers that cite each source and reading for the problem by name. That is being done now, and the results will appear here and on arXiv as it finishes.

The framework itself, with the memory, the recall, the consolidation and the benchmark used to measure whether the brain actually helps, will be released under a documented provenance audit. Soon means when that audit is clean, not before.
